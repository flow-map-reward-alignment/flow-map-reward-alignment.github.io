How to Align Your Flow

Optimal Control

Jerry Huang and Nicholas M. Boffi · October 2026

Reinforcement learning has helped make language models such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek-R1 better at following instructions and solving reasoning problems. How should we use reward feedback to improve image and video generators?

Many of these generators, including Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX, and MiniMax H3, are built on flow matching. They learn to transform noise into a sample by following a continuous trajectory. We would like to improve their outputs through reward feedback while preserving the capabilities learned during pretraining.

There are two ways to do this. Guidance changes the sampling process at test time and leaves the model untouched. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights in an additional training phase. Both face the same design question, which is how to measure departure from the pretrained generator.

The standard answer leads to an odd situation. A flow generates samples by following a deterministic trajectory, yet the common methods for aligning it introduce stochastic dynamics to make a KL-regularized objective tractable. Is KL really the right objective for aligning flows?

In two recent papers, Flow Map Reward Guidance (FMRG) for guidance and Wasserstein-Tilted Flow Maps (WTF) for fine-tuning, we measure departure through the motion of the flow itself. This gives a transport-based regularizer that turns reward alignment into a deterministic optimal control problem.

Flow maps make this formulation particularly useful. By predicting future states along a trajectory, they provide the information needed to guide and fine-tune the generator efficiently. This gives us a reason to care about flow maps beyond their ability to accelerate inference.

Why is aligning a flow so awkward?

Let $r$ be a reward function that scores generated samples, such as a learned model of human preference. The standard objective for reward alignment is KL-regularized reward maximization,

$$\max_{\nu}\ \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[r(x)] - \mathrm{KL}(\nu \,\|\, \rho_1), \qquad \tilde\rho_1(x) \propto e^{\lambda r(x)}\, \rho_1(x),$$

where $\rho_1$ is the distribution of the pretrained model and $\lambda$ sets the strength of the reward. Its solution is the reward-tilted distribution $\tilde\rho_1$, which reweights the base distribution so that samples with high reward become more likely.

For an autoregressive language model this objective is convenient. The log-probability of a sequence is a sum of token log-probabilities, so the KL to the reference model can be estimated from samples, and sampling already consists of stochastic token decisions, which is what policy-gradient methods need. For a flow, both guidance and fine-tuning become harder.

Guidance toward the tilt needs an estimate of the reward a partially generated sample will eventually reach, averaged over the randomness of the rest of generation. That estimate comes from rollouts of an auxiliary stochastic process, and the exponential in the tilt lets a few high-reward rollouts dominate it. Multi-particle methods such as sequential Monte Carlo can target the tilt, but they need many particles and many integration steps, and exact sampling of the tilt can be computationally intractable even for simple quadratic rewards (Moitra et al., 2026).

The exact tilt correction

Sampling the tilt exactly adds to the velocity a correction proportional to $\nabla_x \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda r(X_1)} \mid X_t = x]$, where $X$ follows an auxiliary memoryless stochastic process whose marginals at every time match those of the pretrained model, and the proportionality coefficient depends on the noise level of that process. The expectation runs over all stochastic continuations of the current state, which is why it has to be estimated by simulation.

Fine-tuning runs into a different problem. The likelihood of a flow requires integrating the divergence of its velocity along each trajectory, which makes the KL between pretrained and fine-tuned outputs expensive to evaluate. The tractable alternative used by common methods is a KL between path distributions, but changing a deterministic trajectory makes the old and new path laws singular, so that path-space KL is infinite. Methods such as Adjoint Matching and Flow-GRPO therefore introduce stochastic dynamics. They convert the flow into a diffusion with the same marginals, fine-tune the diffusion, and convert the result back into a flow.

If the generator moves samples along a deterministic flow, we can instead measure departure from the pretrained model through that motion.

A different notion of closeness

A flow generates by moving samples. Each noise sample $x_0 \sim \rho_0$ follows the pretrained velocity $\dot x_t = b_t(x_t)$ until it becomes a sample $x_1$. Suppose we modify the pretrained velocity by adding a control $u_t$. Following the pretrained flow requires no control. Departing from it costs the squared size of the intervention, accumulated along the trajectory. We then maximize the terminal reward while paying for this effort.

$$\max_{u}\ \mathbb{E}_{x_0\sim\rho_0}\Big[\lambda\, r(x_1^u) - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \lVert u_t(x_t^u)\rVert^2\, dt\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \dot x_t^u = b_t(x_t^u) + u_t(x_t^u)$$

This is a deterministic optimal control problem, and its solution is again a single deterministic flow with velocity $b_t + u_t^*$. No auxiliary diffusion appears anywhere. It is the same control problem that FMRG proposed for guidance, where it is solved one trajectory at a time.

WTF shows what this objective means at the level of distributions. The least control effort needed to steer the pretrained flow from a point $x$ to a point $y$ defines a cost $c_b(x, y)$, and the optimal transport cost $\mathcal{T}_b(\rho_0, \nu)$ built from it measures the least effort needed to reach a terminal distribution $\nu$. Maximizing reward regularized by $\mathcal{T}_b$ in place of the KL has the same optimal value as the control problem above. Although $\mathcal{T}_b$ is written with the noise distribution $\rho_0$, it measures departure from the pretrained generator, because its cost contains $b$ and following $b$ costs nothing. The only distribution reachable at zero cost is the pretrained one.

$$c_b(x, y) = \inf_{\omega_0 = x,\ \omega_1 = y}\ \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \lVert \dot\omega_t - b_t(\omega_t) \rVert^2\, dt, \qquad \mathcal{T}_b(\rho_0,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho_0,\nu)}\int c_b(x,y)\,\pi(dx\,dy)$$

With $b\equiv 0$, the regularizer reduces to $\tfrac12 W_2^2(\rho_0,\nu)$. With a nontrivial $b$ it measures the effort required beyond what the pretrained model already does.

Replacing the KL with $\mathcal{T}_b$ changes what the optimum does. The problem decomposes over samples, so each noise sample is transported to the endpoint with its best tradeoff between reward and steering cost. The KL tilt instead reweights the base density according to reward. One consequence is easy to state. If two points have the same reward, any tilt multiplies both of their densities by the same factor $e^{\lambda r}$, so their density ratio never changes, whereas transport can move mass between them.

The difference matters most when high reward lies where the base model puts little mass. Both distributions below are exact population optima, computed without training, so the comparison isolates the effect of the objective. Hold the reward scale fixed and drag the reward location into the tail of the base distribution. While the reward sits near the base, both objectives raise the expected reward. In the tail, the KL tilt has less and less mass there to reweight and its expected reward falls toward the base, while the transport optimum keeps moving samples into the high-reward region. The same value of $\lambda$ sets a different strength under each regularizer, so the example shows how the optima differ in kind.

Why flow maps?

Distribution matching and adversarial distillation also produce strong one-step generators. If fast sampling were the whole story, it would not be clear what learning a flow map adds.

A flow model generates samples by following a velocity field from noise to data. Its flow map $X_{s,t}$ sends a state at time $s$ to the state these dynamics reach at time $t$, so $X_{s,t}(x_s) = x_t$ along every trajectory. A learned flow map approximates this operator in one network evaluation. One-step sampling uses $X_{0,1}$, while alignment also uses maps that start at intermediate times.

This raises a puzzle. A flow map is trained to skip the trajectory, while the control problem is defined along it, so there is little reason to expect the map to help. It does, because it makes the flow easy to control.

Controlling a flow means steering samples while they are being generated, and that asks for more than a sample. To steer a half-generated sample, we need to know where it is heading, how its destination changes when we change the current state, and what happens along the way, since the control cost accumulates at intermediate times. An endpoint generator that maps noise to a sample does not supply these transitions from the intermediate states of the original flow. A flow map answers each question with one evaluation.

Other generators can also be differentiated, seed-optimized and fine-tuned with rewards, and training recipes such as distribution matching can be applied to models that represent flow maps. The advantage here is access to the dynamics that the control formulation needs. For guidance, the flow map predicts where the current sample is heading and how a change now would change its final reward. For fine-tuning, it supplies the future states needed to evaluate and improve the current control.

Guidance by looking ahead

At test time, FMRG solves the control problem along a single trajectory. Its guidance takes three steps. Predict the endpoint from the current state with the pretrained flow map, evaluate the reward there, and differentiate to find how changing the current state would improve that future reward.

$$u_t(x) = \lambda\, \nabla X_{t,1}(x)^\top\, \nabla r\big(X_{t,1}(x)\big)$$

The whole signal costs one backpropagation through a single flow-map evaluation. It is greedy, because the lookahead assumes no further intervention, even though guidance is applied again at later steps.

The exact optimal control has the same form, with the flow map of the controlled dynamics in place of the pretrained one. Along an optimal trajectory $x_t^*$, holding the control fixed when differentiating the trajectory, the maximum principle gives

$$u_t^* = \lambda\, \nabla X^{u^*}_{t,1}(x_t^*)^\top\, \nabla r\big(X^{u^*}_{t,1}(x_t^*)\big).$$

This is circular, because the controlled flow map describes the trajectory we are trying to construct. Using the pretrained map instead is justified in two complementary ways. It approximates the optimal control with error $O(\lambda^2)$ as $\lambda \to 0$, and for any $\lambda$ it is the optimal correction when the control acts only in a vanishingly short window around the current time. In practice FMRG alternates two operations. A flow-map step approximates a finite-time transition of the pretrained dynamics in one evaluation, and a guidance step applies the correction above.

The same view gives DPS an interpretation through deterministic control. In the linear-interpolant setting, the denoiser's prediction of the endpoint, the conditional mean, coincides with a single Euler step of the flow, while a flow map predicts where the deterministic trajectory actually ends. Replacing the flow-map endpoint and its derivative with the Euler approximation recovers the DPS signal, so DPS sits at the coarsest level of a hierarchy whose finest level is exact optimal control.

FMRG comes in two variants. FMRG-J differentiates through the flow map as above, and FMRG-E uses the reward gradient at the predicted endpoint without the flow-map Jacobian, which saves memory. In style guidance, both flow-map variants transfer the reference style, while their denoiser-based counterparts miss it or add artifacts.

Guidance strength and early stopping, which applies guidance only on an early portion of the trajectory, control over-optimization of the reward. Because each step uses an accurate lookahead, guidance needs few network function evaluations (NFEs), the number of model calls per sample. On GenEval, which scores compositional accuracy with object detectors that are independent of the reward used for guidance, FMRG reaches the 0.77 of flow map trajectory tilting, a multi-particle sampler that targets the reward tilt, with 20 NFEs where that method uses 1400. That is 70× fewer network evaluations at matched accuracy. On latent inverse problems, FMRG outperforms DPS, FlowDPS and FlowChef with up to 10× fewer.

Fine-tuning by learning the control

The two methods meet at the value function. With no future control, the value of a state is the reward it will reach, $V_t^0(x) = \lambda\, r(X_{t,1}(x))$, and its gradient is exactly the FMRG-J signal. FMRG looks ahead under the pretrained dynamics. WTF evaluates and improves a control that changes during training, so its value must also count the control cost still to come.

Guidance solves the control problem anew for every sample. Fine-tuning learns the optimal control once and writes it into the model. The value of a control at a state is the reward its trajectory reaches minus the control cost still to spend,

$$V^u_t(x) = \lambda\, r\big(X^u_{t,1}(x)\big) - \frac{1}{2}\int_t^1 \lVert u_\tau(X^u_{t,\tau}(x))\rVert^2\, d\tau, \qquad u^*_t = \nabla_x V^*_t$$

Its gradient points to changes of the state that improve this remaining outcome, and the optimal control is exactly the gradient of the optimal value function. WTF improves its control by policy iteration. It freezes a reference control $\bar u$, estimates its value gradient, fits an updated control to that target, and refreshes the reference. In the idealized population setting, a performance-difference identity shows that each such update improves the objective, and that the iteration can stop only at the optimal control.

Updating the control changes its trajectories, so a map of the original dynamics no longer predicts the right futures. WTF therefore learns the velocity and continually self-distills its flow map together, and the updated map $X^{\bar u}$ supplies the future states for the next update. From a state $x_t$, the value of the reference control takes two transitions of that map to estimate.

$$\widehat V(t, x_t) = \lambda\, r(x_1) - \frac{1-t}{2}\, \lVert \bar u_\tau(x_\tau)\rVert^2, \qquad \tau\sim\mathrm{U}[t,1],\quad x_\tau = X^{\bar u}_{t,\tau}(x_t),\quad x_1 = X^{\bar u}_{\tau,1}(x_\tau)$$

Sample $\tau$ uniformly between $t$ and $1$. The transition from $x_t$ to $x_\tau$ gives the state where the control cost is evaluated, and multiplying that cost by $1 - t$ estimates the remaining integral. The transition from $x_\tau$ to $x_1$ gives the endpoint where the reward is evaluated. Differentiating the estimate with respect to $x_t$ gives the regression target for the control. With an exact reference map the estimate is unbiased, and its only randomness is the one-dimensional time sample, in contrast to the high-dimensional expectations behind the stochastic value functions of the reward tilt. In practice the remaining error comes from the learned map.

The map learned to accelerate sampling therefore also supplies what is needed to improve the model, without a learned critic or a full rollout. We call this simulation-free reinforcement learning, in the sense that the rollouts it needs are amortized by the flow map.

Keeping the velocity and the map consistent matters. Backpropagating the terminal reward directly through the map lets the reward improve through the endpoint map while the velocity fails to learn the corresponding dynamics. WTF instead regresses the velocity onto the estimated value gradient and distills the map from that velocity. The large-scale image experiments use a Euclidean variant of the reward gradient, which keeps the map's endpoint prediction and drops its Jacobian from the reward term. In the idealized setting, every fixed point of the loss is the optimal control together with its flow map. The output is a fine-tuned flow map, so the same checkpoint generates at any budget from one step to many, without post-hoc distillation.

WTF reaches the peak rewards of Flow-GRPO and Adjoint Matching, both KL-regularized methods that rely on stochastic sampling, with 280× and 47× less fine-tuning compute.

Looking Forward

These two papers suggest that the objective for reward alignment deserves to be reconsidered alongside the models we want to align. Measuring departure through the pretrained dynamics leads to a deterministic control problem that supports both guidance and fine-tuning. The choice of regularizer changes both the distribution we seek and the computations needed to reach it.

From this perspective, flow maps emerge as a foundational primitive for efficient reward alignment. Their ability to predict future states makes it possible to evaluate and improve a generative trajectory without simulating every intermediate step. FMRG uses this capability for guidance at test time, while WTF uses it to learn an improved generator. Continual self-distillation preserves this capability as the model changes, so the fine-tuned model remains a flow map that supports both few-step generation and further alignment.

We believe this role extends beyond the methods developed here. As reward feedback becomes a larger part of training generative models, efficiently evaluating the consequences of the model's own decisions becomes increasingly important. Flow maps provide precisely this capability. Together, these results highlight a dual role for flow maps as primitives for fast generation and efficient control. We hope they encourage further exploration of flow maps and the alignment objectives that make use of their structure.
