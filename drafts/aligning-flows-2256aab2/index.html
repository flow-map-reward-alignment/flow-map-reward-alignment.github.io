How to Align Your Flow
with
Optimal Control

Jerry Huang and Nicholas M. Boffi · October 2026

Flow-based generative models produce excellent samples, and in practice we rarely want just any sample. We want images that people prefer, reconstructions that agree with a measurement, or outputs that follow a prompt more closely. These criteria are usually encoded as a reward $r$, and reward alignment asks how to raise the reward of generated samples while keeping what the pretrained model already does well.

There are two ways to do this. Guidance changes the sampling process at inference time and leaves the model untouched. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights in an additional training phase. Both face the same design question, which is how to measure departure from the pretrained generator. In two recent papers, Flow Map Reward Guidance (FMRG) for guidance and Wasserstein-Tilted Flow Maps (WTF) for fine-tuning, we argue that the standard answer to this question fits flows poorly, and that a different answer turns alignment into a deterministic optimal control problem. Flow maps are what make that problem efficient to solve, because they make a flow easy to control as well as fast to sample.

Why is aligning a flow so awkward?

The standard objective for reward alignment is KL-regularized reward maximization,

$$\max_{\nu}\ \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[r(x)] - \mathrm{KL}(\nu \,\|\, \rho_1), \qquad \tilde\rho_1(x) \propto e^{\lambda r(x)}\, \rho_1(x),$$

where $\rho_1$ is the distribution of the pretrained model and $\lambda$ sets the strength of the reward. Its solution is the reward-tilted distribution $\tilde\rho_1$, which reweights the base distribution so that samples with high reward become more likely.

A brief history of the KL objective

KL-regularized reward maximization became the default through language models. Early work on fine-tuning language models from human preferences maximized a learned reward while penalizing the KL divergence from the pretrained model, so that the policy could not exploit the reward model by drifting away from natural text (Ziegler et al., 2019; Stiennon et al., 2020). The same objective trained InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022). Its optimum has a closed form, the pretrained distribution reweighted by the exponentiated reward (Korbak et al., 2022), which Direct Preference Optimization turned into a simple training loss (Rafailov et al., 2023).

Diffusion and flow models inherited this objective. Reinforcement learning methods for diffusion treat the denoising chain as a sequence of stochastic actions (Black et al., 2023), with a KL penalty to the pretrained model (Fan et al., 2023). Continuous-time formulations cast KL-regularized fine-tuning as entropy-regularized stochastic optimal control (Uehara et al., 2024), and Adjoint Matching showed that reaching the reward tilt from a flow model requires a particular memoryless noise schedule (Domingo-Enrich et al., 2024). Flow-GRPO converts the deterministic sampler into an equivalent stochastic one so that policy-gradient methods can explore (Liu et al., 2025).

For an autoregressive language model this objective is convenient. The model assigns an exact probability to every sequence as a sum of token log-probabilities, so the KL to the reference model can be estimated from samples, and sampling already consists of stochastic token decisions, which is what policy-gradient methods need.

For a flow the same objective is much harder to use. The tilt is hard to sample. Exact sampling adds to the dynamics a correction $\nabla_x \log \mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda r(x_1)} \mid x_t = x]$, an expectation over an auxiliary stochastic process that runs to the end of generation, and the exponential lets a few high-reward samples dominate any estimate of it. Recent work shows that sampling the tilt is computationally intractable even for simple rewards, and multi-particle methods such as sequential Monte Carlo need many particles and many integration steps. Fine-tuning runs into a second problem. The likelihood of a flow requires integrating the divergence of its velocity along each trajectory, and changing a deterministic trajectory makes the KL between the old and new path distributions infinite. Common KL-based fine-tuning methods such as Adjoint Matching and Flow-GRPO therefore introduce stochastic dynamics. They convert the flow into a diffusion, fine-tune the diffusion, and convert the result back into a flow, even as the field moves toward deterministic samplers and flow maps that generate in a few steps.

Is KL really the right objective for aligning flows?

A different notion of closeness

A flow generates by moving samples. Each noise sample $x_0 \sim \rho_0$ follows the pretrained velocity $\dot x_t = b_t(x_t)$ until it becomes a sample $x_1$. So we can measure departure from the pretrained model through that motion. Suppose we modify the pretrained velocity by adding a control $u_t$. Following the pretrained flow requires no control. Departing from it costs the squared size of the intervention, accumulated along the trajectory. We then maximize the terminal reward while paying for this effort.

$$\max_{u}\ \mathbb{E}_{x_0\sim\rho_0}\Big[\lambda\, r(x_1^u) - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \lVert u_t(x_t^u)\rVert^2\, dt\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \dot x_t^u = b_t(x_t^u) + u_t(x_t^u)$$

This is a deterministic optimal control problem, and its solution is again a single deterministic flow with velocity $b_t + u_t^*$. No auxiliary diffusion appears anywhere. It is the same control problem that FMRG proposed for guidance, where it is solved one trajectory at a time.

WTF shows what this objective means at the level of distributions. The least control effort needed to steer the pretrained flow from a point $x$ to a point $y$ defines a cost $c_b(x, y)$, and the optimal transport cost $\mathcal{T}_b(\rho_0, \nu)$ built from it measures the least effort needed to reach a terminal distribution $\nu$. Maximizing reward regularized by $\mathcal{T}_b$ in place of the KL has the same optimal value as the control problem above.

$$c_b(x, y) = \inf_{\omega_0 = x,\ \omega_1 = y}\ \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \lVert \dot\omega_t - b_t(\omega_t) \rVert^2\, dt, \qquad \mathcal{T}_b(\rho_0,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho_0,\nu)}\int c_b(x,y)\,\pi(dx\,dy)$$

With $b\equiv 0$, the regularizer reduces to $\tfrac12 W_2^2(\rho_0,\nu)$. With a nontrivial $b$ it measures the effort required beyond what the pretrained model already does.

Replacing the KL with $\mathcal{T}_b$ changes what the optimum does. The problem decomposes over samples, so each noise sample is transported to the endpoint with its best tradeoff between reward and steering cost. The KL tilt instead reweights the base density according to reward. One consequence is easy to state. If two points have the same reward, any tilt multiplies both of their densities by the same factor $e^{\lambda r}$, so their density ratio never changes, whereas transport can move mass between them.

The difference matters most when high reward lies where the base model puts little mass. In one dimension both optima can be computed exactly. Hold the reward scale fixed and drag the reward location outward. While the reward sits near the base, both objectives raise the expected reward. As it moves into the tail, the KL tilt has less and less mass there to reweight and its expected reward falls toward the base, while the transport optimum keeps moving samples into the high-reward region.

Why flow maps?

This brings us to a question we found harder to answer than we expected. Distribution matching and adversarial training also turn a pretrained flow into a generator that samples in one step. If fast sampling were the whole story, it would not be clear what a flow map adds. Our answer is that a flow map makes the flow easy to control.

Controlling a flow means steering samples while they are being generated, and that asks for more than a sample. To steer a half-generated sample, we need to know where it is heading, how that destination changes when we nudge the sample, and what happens along the way, since the control cost accumulates at intermediate times. A generator that maps noise to a sample answers none of these questions from an intermediate state. A flow map $X_{s,t}$ answers all three. It approximates the transition of the pretrained flow between any two times, $X_{s,t}(x_s) \approx x_t$, in a single evaluation.

Where is the sample heading?

$X_{t,1}(x)$

the endpoint from the current state, where the reward is evaluated

How does that depend on the sample?

$\nabla X_{t,1}(x)$

how a change to the current state moves the endpoint

What happens along the way?

$X_{t,\tau}(x)$

intermediate states, where the control cost is paid

These quantities appear in the solution of the control problem itself. Along an optimal trajectory $x_t^*$, the optimal control satisfies

$$u_t^* = \lambda\, \nabla X^{u^*}_{t,1}(x_t^*)^\top\, \nabla r\big(X^{u^*}_{t,1}(x_t^*)\big),$$

where $X^{u^*}$ is the flow map of the controlled dynamics. The control at time $t$ is the reward gradient at the endpoint, pulled back through the map to the current state. For guidance, a flow map supplies this lookahead for deciding how to steer the current sample. For fine-tuning, it supplies the future states needed to evaluate and improve the current control. In both cases the flow map turns control into a handful of network evaluations, and that is what makes alignment cheap.

Guidance by looking ahead

At inference time, FMRG solves the control problem along a single trajectory. The exact control is circular, because it needs the flow map of the controlled dynamics, which is the trajectory we are trying to construct. FMRG replaces it with the pretrained flow map.

$$u_t(x) = \lambda\, \nabla X_{t,1}(x)^\top\, \nabla r\big(X_{t,1}(x)\big)$$

The signal has three parts. The flow map predicts the final sample from the current state. The reward gradient says how that final sample should change to improve the reward. Differentiating through the flow map says how to change the current state to produce that improvement. The whole signal costs one backpropagation through a single flow-map evaluation. The lookahead assumes that the rest of the trajectory follows the pretrained flow. After each correction we look ahead again from the updated state, so the procedure is greedy, with every correction using the future predicted by the pretrained dynamics.

This signal is justified in two complementary ways. It is the optimal control to second order as $\lambda \to 0$, and for any $\lambda$ it is the optimal correction when the control acts only in a short window around the current time. In practice FMRG alternates two operations. A flow-map step approximates a finite-time transition of the pretrained dynamics in one evaluation, and a guidance step looks ahead with the flow map to the endpoint, evaluates the reward there, and corrects the current state.

The same view organizes existing guidance methods into a hierarchy. Exact optimal control uses the controlled flow map. FMRG uses the pretrained flow map. DPS replaces the flow-map endpoint with a single Euler step, the posterior mean, and so sits at the coarsest level of the hierarchy, together with methods such as FlowDPS and FlowChef that differ mainly in their guidance weights. Read this way, DPS is a one-step approximation of a control problem, rather than an approximation of the reward tilt. FMRG comes in two variants. FMRG-J backpropagates through the flow-map Jacobian as in the signal above, and FMRG-E drops the Jacobian and applies the reward gradient at the predicted endpoint directly, which saves memory. The difference across the hierarchy is visible in style guidance, where both flow-map variants transfer the reference style and their denoiser-based counterparts miss it or add artifacts.

Because each step uses an accurate lookahead, guidance needs very few network evaluations. On GenEval, FMRG matches a multi-particle sampler that targets the reward tilt with 70× fewer network evaluations, and on latent inverse problems it outperforms DPS, FlowDPS and FlowChef with up to 10× fewer.

Fine-tuning by learning the control

Guidance solves the control problem anew for every sample. Fine-tuning learns the optimal control once and writes it into the model. WTF does this with the value function, the reward still to come from a state minus the control energy still to spend.

$$V^u_t(x) = \lambda\, r\big(X^u_{t,1}(x)\big) - \frac{1}{2}\int_t^1 \lVert u_\tau(X^u_{t,\tau}(x))\rVert^2\, d\tau, \qquad u^*_t = \nabla_x V^*_t$$

The value function measures how well the current control will do from a given state, counting both the reward to come and the control effort still to spend. Its gradient points to the changes of that state that improve this remaining outcome, and the optimal control is exactly the gradient of the optimal value function. Policy iteration uses this to build a better control, evaluates the new control, and repeats. WTF runs policy iteration. It fits the trainable control to the value gradient of a frozen copy of itself, then uses the result as the next reference. In the idealized population setting, a performance-difference identity shows that each such update improves the objective, and that the iteration can stop only at the optimal control.

The expensive part of this loop is the value. Estimating it for a stochastic process means rolling out the auxiliary diffusion to the final time and averaging over the noise. Here the flow map supplies the needed states with jumps. Sampling one intermediate time $\tau$ between $t$ and $1$ gives an unbiased estimate that takes two flow-map transitions in place of a multistep rollout.

$$\widehat V(t, x) = \lambda\, r(x_1) - \frac{1-t}{2}\, \lVert \bar u_\tau(x_\tau)\rVert^2, \qquad \tau\sim\mathrm{U}[t,1],\quad x_\tau = X_{t,\tau}(x),\quad x_1 = X_{\tau,1}(x_\tau)$$

The estimator follows from a simple Monte Carlo idea. The reward needs only the endpoint. The remaining control cost is an integral over time, and we estimate it by sampling one time $\tau$ uniformly in $[t, 1]$, evaluating the control cost there, and multiplying by the length $1 - t$ of the remaining interval. The flow map reaches the sampled intermediate state and then the endpoint in two transitions. Averaged over the sampled time, this recovers the integral exactly when the transitions follow the reference dynamics.

The only randomness in this estimate comes from a one-dimensional time integral, in contrast to the high-dimensional expectations behind the stochastic value functions of the reward tilt. Its gradient gives the regression target for the control.

There is a catch. As the control improves, the trajectories change, and a flow map distilled only from the original model would predict the wrong futures. WTF therefore updates the velocity and the flow map together. Regressing the velocity onto the estimated value gradient improves the control, and self-distillation teaches the map to follow the updated velocity, so the next iteration can evaluate the new control with the same two jumps. Keeping the two in step matters. Backpropagating the terminal reward directly through the map lets the endpoint map gain reward while the velocity fails to learn the corresponding dynamics. In the idealized setting, every fixed point of the loss is the optimal control together with its flow map.

The output is a fine-tuned flow map, so the same checkpoint generates at any budget from one step to many, without a separate distillation stage. We call this simulation-free reinforcement learning, since the rollouts it needs are amortized by the flow map.

The efficiency gain is large. WTF reaches the peak rewards of Flow-GRPO and Adjoint Matching, both KL-regularized methods that rely on stochastic sampling, with 280× and 47× less training compute.

At the end of the same budget, WTF also reaches a higher reward with comparable diversity, and keeps it at four steps.

Takeaway

Every alignment method starts from a choice of what it means to stay close to the pretrained model. That choice is easy to treat as fixed, because the KL objective arrived with mature tools and a clean closed form. But the objective decides which quantities must be computed, and so which algorithms are practical. For flows, choosing KL turned alignment into a problem about stochastic processes, and much of the effort went into building those processes.

Matching the objective to the model changes what is hard. A flow moves samples, so we measured closeness by how much that motion has to change. The resulting problem lives on the flow, its solution is expressed through the flow map, and the objects we built to sample faster turn out to be what makes alignment cheap.

We expect this to hold beyond these two papers. Accelerated samplers such as flow maps are infrastructure for post-training, and an objective adopted from one kind of model deserves a second look when the model we train has a different structure.
